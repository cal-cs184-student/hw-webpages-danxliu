Overview

In this project, I build out the key components of the raytracing pipeline. I start by implementing the ability to create a ray from the camera through a given pixel, and calculating where it hits the scene using the triangle and sphere intersection equations. Next, I optimized the ray intersection checks by storing objects in a bounding volume hierarchy instead of iterating through every single object in the entire scene, improving the performance. To add proper lighting and scene colors, I implemented the zero and one-bounce lighting algorithms, with both hemisphere sampling and importance sampling the lights. Finally, I implemented adaptive sampling to reduce the amount of sampling required for each pixel if we estimate that the pixel color converged.

Part 1: Ray Generation and Scene Intersection

Ray Generation and Primitive Intersections

To draw images using ray tracing, a few steps need to be implemented. We need to be able to shoot multiple rays from a camera and through a pixel on the image plane and determine its intersection point on an object in the scene. Afterwards, we can average the values of the resulting intersection points to get the color of the pixel the rays were shot through.

The first step is creating a new ray object, representing by its origin and direction. We are given the normalized image coordinates \(x, y\) in 2D. We can convert the normalized image coordinates to camera space coordinates by transforming it onto the image plane. Normalized image coordinates are in the space \[ \begin{align*} (0, 0)\\ (1, 1) \end{align*} \], and the camera space image plane is in the coordinates \[ \begin{align*} &(-\tan(0.5 \times \text{hFov}), -\tan(0.5 \times \text{vFov}, -1))\\ &(\tan(0.5 \times \text{hFov}), \tan(0.5 \times \text{vFov}), -1) \end{align*} \]. Thus, this transformation is done with the following equations \[ \begin{align*} c_x &= (x - 0.5) * (2.0 * \tan(\text{hFov}) * 0.5)\\ c_y &= (y - 0.5) * (2.0 * \tan(\text{vFov}) * 0.5) \\ c_z &= -1 \end{align*} \]. Afterwards, we can use the given c2w matrix to convert the direction ray from camera space to world space. \[ \text{direction} = \text{c2w} \begin{bmatrix} c_x \\ c_y \\ c_z \end{bmatrix}\]. The origin is simply the pos variable of the camera.

Next, we need to send out ns_aa sample rays for a given pixel \(x, y\) in the normalized image. We can generate a random sample, \(r_x \in [x, x+1], r_y \in [y, y+1]\) using the gridSampler->get_sample() function and adding it to our given pixel. Then, we scale it down to the proper coordinates between \(0, 1\) by dividing it by the buffer width and height. Afterwards, we use our previous function to create rays, camera->generate_ray(), to generate a ray through the random sample and get the global illumination from the ray. After doing this ns_aa times, we take the average and update the sample buffer's pixel.

Finally, we implement detecting intersections. For the triangle intersection, we can use the Möller Trumbore Algorithm, which calculates the triangle Barycentric coordinates and the time \(t\) given the ray and the traingle points. We can use the Barycentric coordinates to both detect the intersection is within the triangle, and to interpolate the normal vectors across the triangles.
For sphere intersection, we can solve for the sphere intersection using the quadratic equation. This gives us two potential times, \(t_1, t_2\). We can then determine the intersection point by calculating the ray position at these times and validate them. We take the smallest valid intersection, and calculate the normals by normalizing the difference between the intersection point and the sphere's origin.
For both intersection methods, we update the ray's max_t to the intersection's t, since it doesn't need to check any intersections past that point.

Triangle Intersection Algorithm

The triangle intersection algorithm I used was the Möller Trumbore Algorithm, which is a simplified equation that solves for a vector \(\begin{bmatrix} t & b_1 & b2 \end{bmatrix}^{\top}\), where \(t\) is the time of intersection and \(b_1, b_2\) are the Barycentric coordinates for the first and second vertices. The general idea for finding the intersection is to determine the ray intersection point with the triangle's plane, then determine if the intersection point is within the triangle's boundaries. Since we are given the Barycentric coordinates of the intersection point, we can easily determine if it is inside the triangle by ensuring that all of the Barycentric coordinates are greater than zero, and their sum is less than one. If this is satisfied, and \(t\) is within the ray's \([r_{\text{min}}, r_{\text{max}}]\), then we have found the intersection between the triangle and the ray. Otherwise, the ray does not hit the triangle.

Part 2: Bounding Volume Hierarchy

BVH Construction Algorithm

To construct the BVH, I chose to use the median shape's centroid for the split point. To determine the best axis to split on, I tried all three axises and calculated the following cost heuristic: \[ \text{Left Surface Area} \times \text{Number of Left Triangles} + \text{Right Surface Area} \times \text{Number of Right Triangles} \] For each axis, I first sorted the items based on their centroids along the given axis using the std::sort function. Afterwards, I chose to split along the axis with the smallest heuristic. Finally, I can recursively call construct_bvh on the two halves that were created during the split, \([\text{start}, \text{median})\) and \([\text{median}, \text{end})\).
Note that we don't need to divide by the total surface area in the heuristic since the initial surface area is the same for the three axis we are comparing.

Part 3: Direct Illumination

Direct Lighting Algorithm

The first one-bounce lighting algorithm naively takes samples from the entire hemisphere and determines the amount of radiance hitting the point in the direction of the camera. We need to solve the following equation: \[ \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^N \frac{fr(p, \omega_j \to \omega_r)L_i(p, \omega_j) \cos \theta_j}{p(\omega_j)} \] The algorithm first involved taking a direction sample using hemisphereSampler->get_sample(), which returns a sample in the unit hemisphere giving us \(\omega_j\). We also need to convert this direction into world space, using the o2w matrix. Afterwards, we create a ray starting from the hit point, going in the sampled direction, and get the intersection point and its emission, giving us \(L_i\). We can get \(fr(p, \omega_j \to \omega_r)\) using the BRDF of the hit point. Since we sample uniformly over the hemisphere, \(p(\omega_j) = \frac{1}{2 \pi}\). Finally, we can calculate \(\cos \theta\) using the dot product between the world incoming vector and the normal vector of the hit point intersection. We take the average of all the samples, and return the result.

The second once-bounce lighting algorithm importance samples from only the lights from the hemisphere. We still need to solve the same equation: \[ \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^N \frac{fr(p, \omega_j \to \omega_r)L_i(p, \omega_j) \cos \theta_j}{p(\omega_j)} \] We iterate through each of the lights. If the light is a point light, we make a single sample in its direction, otherwise we make ns_area_light amount of samples. For each sample, we get the \(L_i\), direction, distance to the light source, and its probability distribution function \(pdf\) using light->sample(). The direction returned is in world space, so we use the w2o matrix to convert it into unit hemisphere space. Again, we get \(fr(p, \omega_j \to \omega_r)\) from the BRDF of the hit point. However, now instead of checking for an intersection for the new sampled ray we send out, we limit its max_t to the distance to the light source. This means that if it doesn't intersect with anything, the light source will reach the object, and we can directly use the \(L_i\) variable we found earlier. Finally, we again take the dot product between the incoming ray and the normal vector at the hit point to get \(\cos \theta_j\). We take the average of all of the samples to get the answer for the single light source. Finally, we return the sum over all of the light sources.

Part 4: Global Illumination

Indirect Lighting Algorithm

The indirect lighting algorithm, implemented in at_least_one_bounce_radiation, involves taking the hit point, sampling a new ray based on the BSDF, and recursively calling the function on the new ray. We set a hard limit on the amount of times we recurse using ray.depth to prevent infinite recursion. Finally, we use Russian Roulette with a probability of continuing at \(0.7\).
We determine the ray to send out using the sample_f function from the intersection's BSDF, which also gives us \(fr\), \(\omega_j\), and \(pdf\). We then pass the ray and its intersection into a recursive call, getting back the sampled radiance into the point. Now, we can multiply the radiance in with \(fr\), \(\omega_j\), and the dot product between the hit point normal and the new outgoing ray, divided by the pdf and \(0.7\).

Part 5: Adaptive Sampling

Adaptive Sampling Algorithm

Adaptive sampling allows us to estimate how close we are to the true illumination based on the sampled standard deviation and mean. If we detect we are close to the true illumination, we can simply stop rendering to improve speed. Given \(s_1\), the sum of sample illuminations, and \(s_2\), the sum of squared sampled illusions, we can calculate sample mean and the sample standard deviation for the current \(n\) samples as follows: \[ \begin{align*} \mu &= \frac{s_1}{n} \\ \sigma &= \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1} * (s_2 - \frac{s_1^2}{n})} \\ I &= 1.96 * \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \end{align*} \] We do this every samplePerBatch iterations to prevent recalculating for every sample. If \( I \leq \text{maxTolerance} \times \mu \), then we say that we are \(95\%\) confident that the actual illuminance falls between the interval \([\mu - I, \mu + I]\). This will allow us to early exit when we are confident enough, potentially saving some samples.
